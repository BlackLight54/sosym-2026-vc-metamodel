# Design Space Exploration of Verifiable Credentials
In this chapter, I argue for approaching the design of (+\^vcs) with systematic methods. I discuss the different characteristics that *generic* and *domain specific* (+\^vcse) may have, and review some existing designs along this spectrum. I present requirement which are amenable to a (+.pgm) process. I argue for a (+\~mda) based approach, in order to systematically satisfy requirements and enable cross-format interoperability. Finally, I discuss the the benefits of systematic model generation and the resulting +DSE process.
## An Emerging design problem
As organizations begin adopting (+\~\^vc) for complex business processes, they face increasingly challenging design decisions about how to structure the claims they want to represent into interrelated (+\~\^vcs), which need to serve multiple verification scenarios with different privacy requirements. These design decisions have an impact on privacy, security, and usability characteristics of the resulting (+\~\^vcse).
Consider the previous example of a financial ecosystem (See ). The system needs to verify claims ranging from basic identity information to detailed financial history, employment records, and existing credit obligations. These claims come from multiple issuers - government agencies, employers, other financial institutions - and vary significantly in their structure, sensitivity, revocation patterb , and interdependence.
The design decisions made when structuring these claims into credential schemas have far-reaching implications - from privacy and verifiability to usability. There are generic and domain specific characteristics and interdependencies between the claims, that often form evaluable patterns. As the number of claims and their interdependencies grow, this *design space* becomes increasingly difficult for designers to navigate manually.
The challenge is further complicated by technical constraints and emerging standards. Different credential formats offer varying privacy features - from simple JSON-LD \[@w3cVC\] to sophisticated ZKP-supporting AnonCreds \[@curran2022anoncreds\]. Designers must consider not just the logical grouping of claims, but also how these groups map to the technical capabilities of different formats, especially when interoperability between formats is required. As (+\^vcse) grow to encompass more issuers, subjects, and verifiers, these design decisions have an increasingly significant impact on the overall system's privacy, efficiency, and trustworthiness.
This emerging complexity in +vcs design creates a need for systematic approaches for exploring and evaluating different schema architectures. Without formal methods to analyze design alternatives, credentials risk being either too coarsely grouped (compromising privacy through unnecessary disclosure and revocation) or too finely separated (increasing complexity and correlation risks). The following sections present my approach to this challenge, demonstrating how +dse techniques can be applied to +vcs design to systematically evaluate these trade-offs and generate "optimal" schema organizations for specific use cases, while also providing interoperability between different +vc formats.
### Application specific usage of VCs
As +vc can represent arbitrary claims, there are many possible and some already explored use-cases where they offer a benefit to existing data transfer systems. As shown by their adoption by the European Union \[@eudiw;@eidas2;@eu_dataspaces;@gaiax\], they are suitable for natural persons and legal entities, and to be used in sector specific use cases with complex domain requirements (*data markets*), as well as generic representation of widely understood and used claims (*electronic identification*).
I want to highlight the latter spectrum, particularly that the generic claim sets have categorically different stakeholder and requirement sets from sector specific ones. They need to be adopted by nation states and inter-govermental organizations, and must satisfy the needs of potentially billions of entities using them rather than the fewer, but still numerous organizations within an industry sector. Of course, there is relation between generic and sector specific design choices (*e.g. KYC processes should accept national eIDs*), with generic choices influencing sector specific ones.
I argue that the design process I lay out in this work is suitable for both, but practically, because the adoption process in business sectors is more hierarchical than democratic, and because the requirement sets - even though more complex - are more cleanly defined, the benefits of my process are more pronounced in sector specific +vcse.

### Relevant state of the art
Surveying existing publications, there is yet to be a systematic process for designing +vcs or +vcse. Still, there is inspiration to be gained from published large-scale, manual design processes.
From the generic end of the previously defined spectrum, there is the \*European Union Digital Identity Wallet \[@eudiw\], which is a framework for member states to implement +ssi-like wallet applications and identity systems in the EU. One key design consideration they take is to designate a special set of claims - the "PID" or "Personal Identification Data" credential - which are under much higher scrutiny from a privacy and truthfulness perspective, because the unwanted disclosure or correlation of the claim set poses a paramount risk for the individual. Because of these higher guarantees, they can be even be used in contexts which requires such high level of assurance, such as the tax-collection or medicine. The key takeaway is that defining risk for unwanted disclosure or correlation for a claim or set of claims, and making design decisions based on that risk enables higher levels of assurance and the mitigation of those risks.
On the other end of the spectrum, I found a few proposed +vcse for specific industries. First, there has been a proposal for a highly scalable operating model for digitization and trust of cross border trade based on verifiable credentials \[@kaushiksrinivasan_edata_2023\], which was showcased by Amazon \[@amazon_vc_for_gov\] as a valuable use-case for governments to adopt. They demonstrate that by using +vc, and carefully considering their design, while incorporating the needs of specific Issuers and Verifiers, they can improve efficiency, and mitigate risks associated with inauthentic goods, while maintaining existing, paper-based business processes.
Second, Garzon et al. \[@garzon_certificates_2024\] prototype a 5G Service-Based Architecture that utilizes +vc instead of traditional X.509 certificates and OAuth2.0 access tokens to authenticate and authorize network functions. In their prototype, they propose a hierarchical model of trust delegation using +vc between Issuers, which decentralizes trust relations when it comes to issuing AuthN and AuthZ +vc. My key takeaways from their research is that even a technically specific field - Telecommunications - can benefit from the adoption of +vc, and that the design of a (+vcse) is not only influenced by the simple design aspects a specific claim poses in and of itself, but also the structures and hierarchies they form.
### Requirements in interrelated Verifiable Credential Schema Ecosystems
As I explained in , there are many design aspects associated with claims within +vc. How claims are divided between between +vc poses diverse risks to Issuers, Holders, and Verifiers alike, as thoroughly discussed by the W3C in their standard \[@w3cVC\]. Some of these risks can be mitigated at design time by defining requirements which makes the harmful or exploitable state unrepresentable. In this section, I show how some of these unwanted scenarios can be avoided by considering how claims are distributed across credentials. As +vc can be modelled as +kg, these distributions can be represented as graph patterns.
### Bundling dependent claims
Consider the following example borrowed from the W3C \[@w3cVC\]:<br>"A university is issuing two +vc to an individual. Each +vc contains two properties that, when combined, indicate the person's"role" in a specific "department." For instance, one +vc pair might designate "Staff Member" in the "Department of Computing," while another could signify "Post Graduate Student" in the "Department of Economics." Atomizing these +vc results in the university issuing four separate credentials to the individual. Each credential contains a single designation: "Staff Member", "Post Graduate Student", "Department of Computing", or "Department of Economics". The Holder might then present the "Staff Member" and "Department of Economics" +vc to a Verifier, which, together, would comprise a *false* claim."
This scenario can be avoided if the pattern of putting a dependent claim in a standalone credential is dis-alowed. We can easily reify this pattern using a predicate in the Partial Graph Model. Specifically, when we have a hierarchical dependence of claims, we can define the pattern that a credential for a child claim always contains the claim directly depended upon. Doing this upwards the whole dependence tree ensures that when presenting the whole tree or the relevant sub-tree, the Holder cannot represent the false claim laid out in this scenario.
### Static-dynamic claims
Another example to consider is when the revocation pattern of the claims within a credential are different. Given a passport credential which contains a person's name - constant throughout their life - and the claim they can travel abroad - revoked periodically. When such credential is put to use, the claims which have a longer "lifespan" are unnecessarily revoked, and could even result in the Holder being unable to present a claim could be considered valid not for the unnecessary revocation. In these cases, where the revocation patterns of the claims mismatch, then they should be put into *separate* credentials. In a sense, this is opposite of the previous example, as this requires that a credential *does not* contain two claims with different revocation patterns.
### The case for an MDA Approach
The design of (+\^vcse) for the purpose of +dse presents unique challenges that align naturally with the principles and benefits of +mda. The choice of +mda emerged from the following three considerations regarding the design of (+\^vcse):
> \[!info\] MDA specific considerations for VCSE design
	- The need for systematic requirement satisfaction
	- The imperative for interoperability between different VC formats
	- The complexity of managing multiple stakeholder needs across different abstraction levels
### Systematically ensuring the satisfaction of requirements
My approach for +dse of (+\^vcs) utilizes the systematic generation of diverse models. Because of this, it is imperative to use an approach which systematically ensures the satisfaction of several, potentially complex requirements. +mda offers several key advantages in this regard:
> \[!example\] Benefits of MDA in systematically meeting requirements
	**Separation of Concerns:**<br>+mda enables the modeling of requirements of the abstraction layers independently , while also systematically ensuring these requirements are met throughout the entire process. This means that:
	- Business requirements can be modeled independently of technical implementation details
	- Privacy requirements can be enforced at the conceptual level before technical decisions are made
	- Security constraints can be verified across different levels of abstraction
	**Traceable Transformations:**<br>The *transformations* defined in an +mda process provide traceability between the model elements across abstraction levels. This traceability is useful for maintaining consistency and managing changes in complex credential ecosystems. Specfically:
	- Each transformation from CIM to PIM to PSM maintains clear linkages
	- Requirements can be traced from business needs to technical implementation
	- Changes in requirements can be systematically propagated through the model chain
	**Validation at Multiple Levels:**<br>MDA's layered approach enables validation of different aspects of the credential ecosystem at the most appropriate level of abstraction:
	- Business rules can be validated at the CIM level
	- Structural constraints can be checked at the PIM level
	- Technical compliance can be verified at the PSM level
### Enabling interoperability between carrying formats
Another compelling benefit of adopting +mda in +vcse design is its natural fit for handling multiple +vc formats. (+mda)'s platform-independent modeling approach allows designers to define the abstract model of a +vcse without being constrained by format-specific requirements. By establishing patterns for abstract requirements at the +pim level, designers can focus on the essential characteristics of the abstract model without getting entangled in the technical details of specific +vc formats. This abstraction enables changes to be made at a conceptual level, ensuring that modifications to credential structure or behavior can be implemented without concern for format-specific constraints.
Clear transformation rules enable the generation of implementations in various formats such as JSON-LD \[@w3cVC\], SD-JWT \[@terbu_sdjwtbased_2024\], and AnonCreds \[@curran2022anoncreds\]. Also, this approach provides natural extensibility - support for new +vc formats can be added by defining new transformation rules without altering the abstract +vc model.
+mda ensures consistent semantics across different +vc formats. This semantic consistency is particularly valuable in complex credential ecosystems where different participants may prefer or require different formats while needing to maintain interoperability.
### The expected benefits of Design Space Exploration
The expected benefits of applying +dse to +vcse design span multiple dimensions, from immediate technical advantages to longer-term ecosystem benefits.
At the most fundamental level, +dse enables systematic exploration of schema partitioning options while ensuring compliance with privacy and security requirements. By generating and evaluating multiple valid designs, it allows designers to discover non-obvious solutions that might be missed through manual design approaches. This is valuable when dealing with complex credential ecosystems where the interaction between different credentials can create subtle privacy implications.
Rather than relying on intuition alone, +dse enables the evaluation of different designs based on specific metrics. For example, designers can measure the granularity of credential schemas - how coarsely or finely they are grouped, or the correlation risks between different credential combinations. This quantitative approach enables the identification Pareto-optimal solutions where neither privacy nor security nor complexity can be improved without degrading the each other.
Also, the systematic nature of +dse provides strong guarantees about requirement satisfaction. Specifically, that all generated designs provably meet the specified constraints, and also that conflicts between requirements are identified early in the design process. This is especially valuable in regulated environments where credential schemas must satisfy complex compliance requirements while maintaining usability.
Perhaps most importantly, +dse provides designers with insights into the design options and their implications. As credentials are generated and evaluated, patterns emerge that help inform future design decisions. This learning aspect is valuable because:
> \[!example\] Insights gained during an iterative DSE process
	- It helps identify implicit constraints that weren't obvious in the initial requirements
	- It reveals unexpected but valid design alternatives
	- It builds institutional knowledge about effective credential schema patterns
In the context of evolving (+\^vcse), these benefits compound over time as designers can leverage insights from previous iterations. +dse naturally supports an iterative design process where:
> \[!example\] Steps of an iterative DSE process
	1. Initial designs are generated and evaluated
	2. Requirements are refined based on evaluation results
	3. New design constraints are added to address discovered issues
	4. The process repeats with a refined design space
This iterative capability is valuable for (+\^vcse) that need to evolve as requirements change or novel use cases emerge.
Through these complementary benefits, +dse shifts the process of credential schema design from relying primarily on expert intuition to a systematic approach, while still preserving the flexibility needed to address novel requirements and use cases. This combination of systematic rigor with design flexibility is valuable in the rapidly evolving field of (+\^vc), where both technical capabilities and real-world applications are rapidly expanding and maturing.
